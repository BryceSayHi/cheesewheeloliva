# Add hist_q4_title / hist_q4_answer lines to add a 4th question (and update the two lines above).
hist_q1_title = What led to the Chicago Riots?
hist_q1_answer = Well the direct trigger was the death of Eugene Williams, a 17-year-old Black teenager whose raft drifted across the "line" that divided the 29th Street beach. A white man on the shore threw rocks at him, preventing him from coming to shore and causing Williams to drown. When a white police officer refused to arrest the man responsible and instead chose to arrest a black man who had thrown a punch at the killer, which turned the crowd's anger into violence.
hist_q1_answer = There was also the Great Migration that brought tens of thousands of Black Southerners to Chicago in search of industrial work and an escape from the Jim Crow laws, and the city's Black population roughly doubled between 1910 and 1920. This growth collided with a rigid housing market. Restrictive covenants, real estate practices, and social pressure confined Black residents to the narrow, overcrowded "Black Belt" on the South Side. As Black families pushed outward in search of decent housing, white neighbors and "improvement associations" resisted, and dozens of Black homes were firebombed in the years before the riot.
hist_q1_answer = Labor competition also added to the tension. Many Black workers had been hired into the stockyards and packing plants during World War I, sometimes as strikebreakers, and most white-dominated unions excluded them or treated them with hostility. When soldiers returned home in 1919 and the wartime economy shrank, white workers feared for their jobs, while Black workers, including veterans who had fought for democracy abroad, were less willing to accept second-class treatment. Institutions only made matters worse. The police were widely seen as biased, and the press often dramatized racial incidents. White neighborhood athletic clubs, which functioned partly as street gangs, organized attacks on Black residents, while politics fueled ethnic and racial rivalries.

hist_q2_title = What happened after the Riots?
hist_q2_answer = The immediate effects were seen in lives and property. Six thousand state militia troops eventually helped restore order, and a heavy rainfall also helped end the violence. Black residents bore the heaviest losses, including most of the homes destroyed. Criminal prosecutions afterward were uneven, and few white rioters faced serious consequences.

hist_q3_title = What did the gov change after the fact?
hist_q3_answer = Governor Frank Lowden appointed the Chicago Commission on Race Relations, which produced the 1922 report The Negro in Chicago. It was a landmark study that documented housing discrimination, employment barriers, and police bias, and it recommended reforms. Yet its recommendations were largely ignored. Restrictive covenants spread, and residential segregation hardened over the following decades, leaving the Black Belt more tightly bound than before. The riots also reshaped Black political and cultural life. Anti-lynching and civil rights organizing gained momentum, and a stronger belief of self-defense took hold, captured in works like Claude McKay's poem "If We Must Die." Within Chicago, Black political organization deepened, eventually contributing to the rise of figures such as Oscar De Priest, who in 1928 became the first Black congressman elected from the North. Distrust between Black workers and organized labor persisted, and many Black workers concluded that employers were a more reliable, if self-interested, ally than white-led unions.

hist_sum = So you see, Al. The 1919 riots were not a spontaneous outburst but the product of migration, housing segregation, labor competition, and institutional bias reaching a breaking point.
